Why is compressing a PDF mostly about compressing its images?

A PDF is text, vector artwork, fonts and embedded bitmaps. Text and vectors are tiny and fonts are typically tens to hundreds of kilobytes. What pushes a file into the tens of megabytes is nearly always scanned pages, photos and screenshots. So ImgIng spends its effort there: find every image object, then decide what each one deserves.

What is “effective DPI” and why measure it?

The same 3000-pixel-wide image needs very different resolution when it fills a page than when it sits at business-card size. ImgIng parses each page’s content streams, tracks the graphics state matrix, and works out the real placed size of every image, then converts that to an effective DPI. Only images above the chosen preset get resampled — anything already below it is left alone rather than damaged a second time. Resampling uses Lanczos-3, which rings and blurs less than the usual bicubic.

Why not turn every image into a JPEG?

JPEG suits photographs, but for screenshots, charts, line art and flat colour it is both larger and blurrier. ImgIng reuses the same content classifier as its image compressor to decide whether each embedded image is a screenshot, illustration, icon, smooth photo or high-texture photo, then races JPEG, indexed palette with Flate, grayscale Flate and keeping the original — and takes whichever is genuinely smallest at that preset’s quality standard. Every decision is listed in the workspace with the before and after size and the measured DPI.

What happens beyond the images?

When rewriting, ImgIng walks the object graph from the document root and drops objects left behind by incremental updates; re-deflates streams that were uncompressed, ASCII-wrapped or LZW-coded; strips private layout-tool data, page thumbnails and XMP metadata; keeps just one copy of byte-identical image and font streams; and repacks everything with object streams and a cross-reference stream. None of that touches a pixel.

What will ImgIng refuse to touch?

Encrypted or permission-protected PDFs are detected and reported so you can unlock them first — ImgIng does not attempt to break protection. JPEG 2000, JBIG2 and CCITT fax images cannot be decoded by browsers and are kept as-is, as are CMYK print JPEGs, which browsers decode unreliably. And any image that does not actually get smaller keeps its original bytes.

Honest limits: ImgIng does not currently subset fonts, so a text-only typeset PDF usually gains only 5%–20% from structural work, and dedicated prepress tools will beat it there. ImgIng’s strong ground is scans, illustrated reports and exported decks — PDFs where images dominate. After compressing, the output is re-read with the same engine and the page count and every page box must match, or the original file is kept instead. Rewriting a digitally signed PDF invalidates the signature.

Frequently asked questions

These visible answers match the current product behaviour and structured data.

Does my PDF get uploaded?

No. Parsing, image re-encoding, structural rewriting and verification all happen in this browser, and no extra runtime needs to be downloaded.

Is the text still searchable and selectable afterwards?

Yes. ImgIng only re-encodes embedded bitmaps and rewrites the file structure. Pages are never flattened into images, so the text layer, bookmarks and links survive.

Why do some PDFs barely shrink?

If the embedded images are already below the chosen DPI and sensibly encoded, or the file is mostly text and vectors, there is nothing to reclaim. ImgIng says so and keeps your original rather than manufacturing a number by lowering resolution.
